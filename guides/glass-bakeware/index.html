Borosilicate or soda-lime: which glass bakeware to buy, and how to read the one you own

As an Amazon Associate we earn from qualifying purchases. The product links on this page are affiliate links.

Two kinds of glass are sold as oven bakeware in America, and only one of them survives a real temperature swing. Borosilicate takes a change of about 330 F before it lets go. Tempered soda-lime takes roughly 100 F. Both are safe for ordinary baking; only one is safe for a frozen dish going into a hot oven. This page lists what is on sale today, sorted by which glass it actually is, and shows how to tell which one is already in your cupboard.

American Pyrex today: what the classics actually are

American pyrex today carries the lower-case logo — the dishes are made in Charleroi and now in Lancaster — and it is tempered soda-lime glass, and has been for decades. That is not an accusation: it is Corning's own published decision. These dishes are fine for ordinary baking. They are not fine for thermal shock. The capitals you see on an old dish, or on a French one, are the other glass.

Read the bottom of the glass

Mark on the glassBuilt by
PYREX in capitals, inside a circle, with 'CG'Corning Glass Works, 1940s-50s: borosilicate. Keep it.
PYREX in capitals + 'MADE IN U.S.A.'Mid-1950s Corning. Circular logo gave way to a straight line in the 1960s.
pyrex in lowercaseSoda-lime glass. Works fine, but never hot oven to cold or wet surface.
PYREX in capitals + 'Made in France'International Cookware, Chateauroux: borosilicate.
Anchor HockingTempered soda-lime by the company's own statement, not borosilicate.
