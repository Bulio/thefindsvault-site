Read the compressor, not the badge: 7 refrigerator names and the 4 companies behind them

September 21, 2026 · 2,544 words · 37 sources

Seven badges, four companies

Walk down the refrigerator aisle and you will count a dozen names. KitchenAid. Maytag. Amana. JennAir. Café. Frigidaire Gallery. Kenmore Elite. Seven badges, seven price tags, seven salesmen telling you these are different machines.

Six of those seven badges are owned by three companies. F1 F3 F6 The seventh is a name that hires a factory, and on those machines the factory is LG. F9

And the part that decides whether the thing is still cold in ten years is not on the badge at all. It is a black lump bolted to the bottom of the cabinet, bought from a short list of suppliers, two of whom pleaded guilty in an American courtroom to fixing what it costs. F18 F19

The three digits that name the factory

Start with the brand that makes this obvious, because it never pretended to be a factory in the first place.

A Kenmore refrigerator has a model number, and the first three digits of that number name the company that actually built the machine. A number starting one-oh-six is a Whirlpool. A number starting two-five-three is a Frigidaire, which is to say an Electrolux. A number starting seven-nine-five or seven-nine-six is an LG. F9 F10

Same badge on the door. Three different factories behind it, depending on who the badge was buying from the year your machine was built.

One honest note about that table, because it matters. Those prefixes are the standard the repair trade uses. F9 F10 They are not published by the company that puts the badge on the door. So when a technician tells you your Kenmore is an LG, he is going by what the trade knows, not by a press release.

And notice what that means in practice. Two Kenmore refrigerators can stand side by side in the same showroom, wearing the same name, and be two entirely different machines with two different parts lists, two different failure patterns and two different repair bills. The badge is the one thing they genuinely share.

Kenmore is the loud example, because it admits by design that it is a name and not a plant. The quiet version of the same thing is the rest of the aisle.

Who owns what, brand by brand

Whirlpool owns KitchenAid, Maytag, Amana and JennAir. F1 Not licenses them. Owns them. And it did not build that collection, it bought it: Maytag arrived in 2006, and Maytag walked in carrying brands it had bought earlier, Amana in 2001 and JennAir back in 1982. F2

So when you stand in front of a KitchenAid and an Amana and decide one of them is the premium choice, you are choosing between two product lines from one company in Michigan, priced apart on purpose.

Frigidaire belongs to Electrolux, the Swedish group, which got it in 1986 when it bought White Consolidated Industries. F3 Electrolux also owns Tappan, Kelvinator and Gibson, three more names an American kitchen would recognise. F4 And Frigidaire Gallery and Frigidaire Professional are not rivals standing next to Frigidaire on the shelf. They are tiers of the same brand from the same owner: the word on the badge changes, the company does not. F5

The one most American buyers still get wrong is General Electric. On the sixth of June, 2016, General Electric completed the sale of GE Appliances to Qingdao Haier, of China, for five point six billion dollars. F6 The deal had been announced that January at five point four billion, and the price went up before it closed. F7 GE Appliances kept the name, kept the Louisville headquarters, and kept the badges: GE, GE Profile, Café, Monogram and Hotpoint. F8

So the Café range that sells itself on American design, and the Monogram that sells itself on American luxury, come from a company whose parent is in Qingdao. That is not an accusation, and it says nothing about whether the machine is good. It is a fact with a date and a press release, and the badge does not carry it.

And LG builds, under its own name and under the Kenmore Elite badge, the machines with the seven-nine-five prefix. F9 LG does not own Kenmore. It is the plant behind those units, which is a different relationship and, as you will see in a minute, it turned out to matter a great deal to the people who bought them.

Seven names. Three owners and one builder. Every one of those lines has a source, and they are all listed in the description.

A refrigerator is a box with a pump in it

Now the part that matters, and the reason this is not just another ownership list.

A refrigerator is a box with a pump in it. The pump is the compressor, and its whole job is to move heat out of the box. Everything else can fail and you still have a fridge: the ice maker, the screen on the door, the water filter, a shelf, a fan, a light. When the compressor goes, you do not have a fridge. You have a cabinet full of warm food and a repair quote that reads like a down payment on a new one.

And almost nobody who buys a refrigerator asks who made that pump. The badge does not say. The spec sheet usually does not say. The salesman genuinely does not know, and is not being evasive when he says so: it is not information the trade puts in front of buyers.

Here is one answer, and it is a stranger one than you would expect. For years one of the largest compressor makers in the world was Embraco. Embraco was not a supplier Whirlpool happened to buy from. Embraco was Whirlpool's own compressor business. F18

Then Whirlpool sold it. On the second of July, 2019, the Japanese motor group Nidec completed the purchase of Embraco for one point zero eight billion dollars. F12 European regulators only cleared that deal on one condition: that Nidec sell off its own existing compressor business, Secop, to somebody else. F13 Think about what that condition tells you. The regulators looked at one company owning both, and decided the market was small enough that it mattered.

And after the sale went through, Embraco kept supplying Whirlpool. F14

Read that sequence one more time. The compressor company changed hands for more than a billion dollars, a regulator had to break up the pieces before it was allowed, and the machines on the shop floor kept getting the same pumps from the same plants. The name on the paperwork moved. The part did not.

The price-fixing case nobody mentions

If a handful of suppliers make the part that decides whether your fridge lives, the obvious question is what stops them agreeing among themselves what that part should cost. For three years, the answer was nothing.

On the fifteenth of November, 2010, Panasonic Corporation pleaded guilty in the United States to fixing the price of refrigerant compressors, and was sentenced to pay a forty-nine point one million dollar criminal fine. F19 A month later, on the sixteenth of December, Embraco North America, then a Whirlpool subsidiary, pleaded guilty in the same conspiracy and was sentenced to pay ninety-one point eight million. F18 Together, one hundred forty point nine million dollars in criminal fines. F35

The conspiracy ran from around the fourteenth of October, 2004, to the end of December 2007. F20 A German subsidiary of Danfoss pleaded guilty separately and paid three million. F21 And this was not a paperwork case about corporate entities: a federal grand jury in Detroit indicted three men by name, the former chief executive of Embraco, the former president of Tecumseh's Brazilian arm, and a Panasonic general manager. F22

Europe ran its own case on the same years and the same part. On the seventh of December, 2011, the European Commission fined four companies a combined one hundred sixty-one million, one hundred ninety-eight thousand euros for a compressor cartel covering the whole European Economic Area. F37 Danfoss took the largest share, ninety million euros. F38 Embraco, fifty-four and a half million. F39 The Italian group ACC, nine million. F41 Panasonic, seven point six six eight million. F40 Tecumseh paid nothing at all: it went to the Commission first and got full immunity for revealing that the cartel existed. F42 That cartel ran from April 2004 to October 2007. F43

Now, what does price-fixing on a component actually do to you, standing in a shop in 2026? Two things, and neither is the obvious one. It does not mean your fridge is defective. It means that for years the price of the single most important part in it was not set by anybody competing for your money, and that the people who build refrigerators were, in one documented case, buying that part from a company they owned. Two continents, two prosecutions, the same years, the same component. And not one line of it is printed on any box in the appliance aisle.

The one place the same pump is documented

Now a caution, and then the one case where I can show you the same pump under two different badges.

Common ownership is not the same thing as a common part. Whirlpool owning four badges is documented. That alone does not prove that a KitchenAid and an Amana run the identical compressor, and I am not going to tell you it does, because I could not find a document that says so brand by brand.

But there is one place where the paperwork says it plainly. LG developed a compressor it calls a linear compressor, and it went into refrigerators sold under LG's own name and into the Kenmore Elite machines LG builds. F17

In 2019 a class action was filed in the federal court of New Jersey, Bentley against LG Electronics U.S.A., case number two nineteen, civil, thirteen five five four. F23 The settlement covered LG refrigerators built between January 2014 and the end of 2017, over a compressor defect that left them not cooling. F24 Owners could claim up to four hundred fifty dollars without producing paperwork, more with it, plus a five-year extended warranty covering the labour on a cooling repair. F25 Claims closed on the eleventh of January, 2021. F26

And here is the line I would frame on the wall if I owned a Kenmore Elite. A firm working the Kenmore side of this states that the settlement excluded Kenmore refrigerators fitted with the same compressor part. F27

Same pump. Same plant. Different name on the door, and a different outcome for the person who paid for it. If you ever wondered whether the badge is just decoration, that is your answer: it is decoration right up to the moment something goes wrong, and then it decides who is covered.

The one brand still building its own

So is there anybody left standing outside this web?

One, that I can actually prove. Sub-Zero refrigerators are built in Madison, Wisconsin, by a company that has stayed in the same family since 1945. F29 And they are built around a design almost nobody else uses: two separate compressors, one for the fresh food side, one for the freezer, each with its own sealed cooling system. F30

That is a real engineering difference rather than a marketing one. Two systems means each side holds its own temperature and its own humidity instead of sharing air through a duct, and it means one failure does not necessarily take the whole machine with it. It is also a large part of why those refrigerators cost what they cost, which is a fair trade to explain out loud rather than a secret.

And I will say exactly what the sources say, not a word more. Sub-Zero designs and builds its refrigerators in Wisconsin with a twin-compressor architecture. F29 F30 I did not find a document saying Sub-Zero manufactures the compressor itself, so I am not saying it.

Which brings me to the part you will not hear often. I went looking for who supplies the compressor in a Liebherr, in a Bosch, a Thermador, a Gaggenau, in the Samsung sold in American stores. I could not find it. BSH's parent, Bosch, is independent of every company named so far, and that much is documented. F11 F32 Liebherr develops and builds in Germany, and that much is documented too. F31 Who makes their compressors, I do not know. A guess dressed up as a fact is worth nothing to you, and it is the reason those brands are not in the list at the top of this video.

Three things to check before you pay

So what do you actually do, standing in the aisle with a card in your hand?

One. Find the model number on the serial plate, inside the door or behind the kick panel, and read it before you read the badge. On a Kenmore, those first three digits name the factory. F9 F10 On everything else the model number is what a parts counter needs, and if a parts counter can find your machine with it, so can you, before you buy rather than after.

Two. Ask the question nobody asks: is the compressor for this model a catalogue part, and who services it locally. Ask it out loud, in the shop, before you pay. A machine you can still get a pump for in ten years is a different purchase from one you cannot, whatever the badge cost, and the answer is usually available in about thirty seconds from a local repair shop.

Three. Read the warranty on the sealed system specifically, not the headline number of years on the front of the brochure. The compressor is the expensive failure, and on most machines the labour costs more than the part. That is exactly why the LG settlement is worth knowing about: what it added was five years of labour on cooling repairs, and labour is where that bill lands. F25

Seven names in the aisle. Three companies that own six of them, and one factory in Korea behind the seventh. F1 F3 F6 F9 One pump under the cabinet, from a supply chain that was prosecuted on two continents for agreeing what that pump should cost. F35 F37 And one brand still designing and building its own, in Wisconsin. F29
