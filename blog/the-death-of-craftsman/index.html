The death of Craftsman: how a $900M deal ended a lifetime guarantee

September 14, 2026 · 1,976 words · 23 sources

The $900 million deal that ended a guarantee

In 1927, Sears paid five hundred dollars for a name it would put on wrenches it did not make. F1 For ninety years, that name was the most trusted in every American garage, backed by a promise nobody else dared to print: break it, bring it back, get a new one, no questions asked. F2 In 2017, the name was sold for about nine hundred million dollars to a company that promised to bring it home. F3 And the factory that company built for that promise closed within three years of its first shift. F4

We are going to show you who actually forged the Craftsman wrench in your toolbox, how one hedge fund turned a lifetime guarantee into collateral, and the three letters stamped on the steel that tell you whether the tool in your hand is the old one or the new one.

If you walked into a Lowe's this week, you probably believe Craftsman is a tool company with its own forges and its own engineers. That is exactly what they want you to believe. Craftsman has never forged a single wrench. It has only ever been a name, and the name has changed hands twice.

Made in America, replaced for life, no receipt

Picture it. It is 1927. F1 A man named Arthur Barrows runs the hardware department at Sears, Roebuck and Company in Chicago, and he wants a line of tools that sounds like it was made by a craftsman, not bought from a catalog. F1 The Marion-Craftsman Tool Company owns the word. Barrows buys it for five hundred dollars. F1 That is the whole origin story: no forge and no founder in a leather apron, just a trademark and a check.

What Sears did next is the part that built the legend. Sears did not build tools. It wrote specifications and found forges that could meet them. For most of the twentieth century, Craftsman wrenches and sockets came out of a handful of American plants: Moore Drop Forging in Springfield, Massachusetts, then Easco, then Danaher, which bought those forges and kept them running. F5 Screwdrivers and pliers came from Western Forge in Colorado Springs. F6 Sears set the price, Sears set the standard, and Sears put its name on the front.

And then Sears did something no one else in the tool business would copy for decades. It printed a guarantee with no expiration date. If a Craftsman hand tool ever failed, for any reason, you brought it back to any Sears store and walked out with a new one. F2 No receipt and no form. A farmer in Nebraska could break a ratchet in 1962 and replace it in 1994. [EX] That promise cost Sears money on every tool that came back. It also built something Sears could never have bought with an ad budget: a generation of mechanics who would not touch anything else.

In 2001, the Harris Poll EquiTrend survey ranked Craftsman number one for quality among all American brands, ahead of companies that actually made things. F7 It was not a tool company. It was a promise, kept at the returns counter of every Sears in America.

The crack: Sears sells the name

Here's the crack. In 2005, a hedge fund manager named Edward Lampert merged Kmart and Sears into a single company, Sears Holdings. F8 He did not come from retail. He came from finance. And a financier does not see a promise at a returns counter. He sees a trademark with a book value that can be borrowed against, and a supplier contract that can be renegotiated.

The first thing to change was not the name. It was the steel. In 2010, Danaher merged its hand tool business with Cooper Tools into a new company called Apex Tool Group, and Apex inherited the Craftsman contract. F9 Within a few years, Apex was moving production of Craftsman mechanics' tools out of its American plants and into factories overseas. F10 The packaging looked the same. The lifetime warranty was still printed on the card. The stamp on the steel changed, and almost nobody looked.

Meanwhile, Sears Holdings was losing money every single year. Between 2011 and 2016 the company lost more than ten billion dollars. F11 And Lampert did what a financier does with an asset when the parent company is bleeding: he put it up for sale. Sears had three brands worth real money, Kenmore, DieHard, and Craftsman. Craftsman was the one somebody would actually pay for.

What a brand is worth once the factory is gone

On January fifth, 2017, Stanley Black & Decker announced it was buying the Craftsman brand from Sears. F3 Read the numbers, because they tell you exactly what was sold. Five hundred twenty-five million dollars in cash at closing. Two hundred fifty million more three years later. And a royalty of between two and a half and three and a half percent on new Craftsman sales, paid to Sears for fifteen years. F3 Roughly nine hundred million dollars, all of it for a word. Not one factory changed hands, because Sears did not own one.

Here is the map, and it is one of the strangest in American retail. Stanley Black & Decker, of New Britain, Connecticut, owns the Craftsman name and sells it through Lowe's, Ace Hardware, and Amazon. F12 Sears kept a license to keep selling Craftsman tools in its own stores for fifteen years, royalty-free. F3 So for years there were two Craftsmans on sale at the same time: the one Lowe's sold, made for Stanley, and the one Sears sold, made by whoever Sears could still pay. Same name, same red letters, different steel, different warranty desk.

Now, the part almost nobody knows. Here is how you tell them apart, and it is stamped into the metal. Old Craftsman wrenches carry a maker's mark next to the name. A "V" in a bar, "-V-", means Moore Drop Forging and later Danaher, forged in the United States. F5 "WF" means Western Forge, Colorado Springs. F6 Modern Craftsman tools mostly say "China" or "Taiwan" in small letters on the shank, or nothing at all. F10 A wrench with the "-V-" mark and no country of origin was almost certainly forged in an American plant that no longer exists. The name tells you nothing. The stamp tells you everything.

And then came the promise. In 2019, Stanley Black & Decker announced it would build a ninety-million-dollar Craftsman plant in Fort Worth, Texas, and bring five hundred jobs home. F13 The plant opened in 2020, with a new line of wrenches marked "Made in USA with global materials." F14 For about two years, it looked like the story might have a different ending.

The plants, the towns, the workers

Now count what it cost. Western Forge, in Colorado Springs, had made Craftsman screwdrivers and pliers for decades, stamped with its own mark. F6 In 2020 the plant closed and a hundred and sixty-four people lost their jobs. F15 The buyer of the brand had changed, the retailer had changed, and the forge that made the actual tools was the piece nobody needed anymore.

Then Fort Worth. In March 2023, less than three years after the ribbon was cut, Stanley Black & Decker announced it was closing the Craftsman plant it had built for that promise, and a hundred and seventy-five workers were told to go home. F4 The company said the products would be made elsewhere. The "Made in USA" wrench line did not survive the plant. F4

Picture the customer. A man in Ohio has his father's Craftsman socket set from 1978, "-V-" stamped on every piece, forty-five years old and still tight. [EX] In 2022 he buys a new Craftsman ratchet at Lowe's to fill a gap in the set. [EX] The first time he leans on it, the pawl skips. He brings it back. The warranty is honored, and he gets another one, made in the same overseas plant as the first. [EX] The promise survived, but the tool it was attached to did not.

And the company that invented the promise? On October fifteenth, 2018, Sears Holdings filed for bankruptcy. F16 In 2019 its remains, including the right to keep selling Craftsman, were bought out of the bankruptcy by Transform Holdco, controlled by the same Edward Lampert. F17 Today there are fewer than a dozen Sears stores left in the United States. F18 The returns counter where the lifetime guarantee lived is, for most Americans, gone.

Which Craftsman tools are still worth buying

So what do you buy instead? You buy a forge, not a name. Three answers, and the reason for each.

One. If you want pliers that will outlive you, Channellock, made in Meadville, Pennsylvania, by the same family since 1886. F19 Forged in the United States, still, every pair. Tongue-and-groove pliers for about twenty dollars that mechanics hand down to their sons. F20

Two. If you want wrenches and sockets at the old Craftsman standard, Wright Tool, of Barberton, Ohio. F21 Every wrench forged and finished in one plant in the United States. They cost more than a Craftsman set at Lowe's, and they are what the old "-V-" Craftsman was trying to be.

Three. If you already own old Craftsman, do not sell it. Read the stamp. A "-V-" wrench or a "WF" screwdriver is an American-forged tool with a lifetime warranty that Stanley Black & Decker still honors at Lowe's. F22 Fill the gaps in the set from the secondhand market, where the old tools cost less than the new ones, and keep them.

Cost per year of use, not price on the sticker. Take the prices on the shelf today: a Wright Tool combination wrench set at about three hundred dollars, a Craftsman set at Lowe's at about a hundred. F23 Over forty years, one Wright set is seven dollars and fifty cents a year. [EX] A Craftsman set that gets replaced under warranty three times still costs you a hundred dollars and three trips, about two dollars and fifty cents a year plus the afternoons. [EX] And a secondhand "-V-" Craftsman set at eighty dollars that never breaks is two dollars a year. [EX] The sticker does not decide it; the forge does.

What to buy instead of a name

Somewhere in America right now, a young man is standing in a Lowe's holding a Craftsman wrench. It has the same red letters as the one in his grandfather's toolbox, the one that has been tight for fifty years. He believes he is buying that. He is buying a word that was sold for nine hundred million dollars, stamped on steel from a plant his grandfather never heard of.

Now you know. Check the stamp and check the forge, and do not pay for a name that stopped making anything a long time ago.
